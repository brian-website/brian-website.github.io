SECTION 1 — HYDROGEN ECONOMY

Hydrogen & the Hydrogen Economy

Hydrogen isn't a new idea — town gas was ~50% H2. What's new is using it as a zero-carbon energy carrier. Here's the basics...

Learning objectives: state key properties of H2 • explain "energy carrier" vs "fuel" • describe current H2 supply/demand • list the barriers to a hydrogen economy.

Energy Use Through the Ages

  1. Ancient — wood burning (heating, cooking). Short transport.
  2. Iron age — coal: steam engines, metallurgy.
  3. Age of oil — hydrocarbons, long-distance transport (pipelines, tankers), ICE, plastics.
  4. Electrification — power plants, mid-range grids, EVs, internet.
  5. Age of hydrogen (coming) — driven by fossil fuel depletion + GHG emissions. Renewables (PV + wind) make electricity, but storage & transport are the problem → H2 as a carrier.
H2 is an energy carrier, NOT a primary fuel. It isn't mined, so you always have to spend energy making it first.

Hydrogen — Key Facts

  • 1 proton + 1 electron (1s1). Oxidation states −1 (hydrides), 0 (H2), +1 (H+).
  • At STP: odourless, colourless, non-toxic, combustible gas. Behaves almost like an ideal gas.
  • Reacts violently with O2, giving a fast flame or an explosion.
  • 10th most abundant element on Earth (by atoms); 75% of the Universe by mass. Found mostly in water, hydrocarbons and crystal hydrates.

What is the "Hydrogen Economy"?

Past/present: H2 is made mostly by steam methane reforming and used mostly for NH3 synthesis (~50%) and hydrocracking in refineries. About 87 Mt (2020), rising to ~120 Mt/yr (2022).

Future: H2 as a zero-carbon energy carrier. That needs infrastructure, sustainable generation, storage & transport, and efficient use.

120 Mt/yr ≈ 3% of global final energy  ⇒  to cover it all: 120 × 30 ≈ 3600 Mt/yr

The Six Big Challenges

  1. Cost — renewable H2 costs 2–3× fossil H2 (oil at $75/bbl, gas at $4–6/GJ).
  2. Lack of differentiation — no established way to tell low-carbon H2 from fossil H2.
  3. No market — not a traded commodity, so there's no price index and little transparency.
  4. Limited infrastructure — only ~4500 km of H2 pipelines worldwide.
  5. Energy losses — every conversion step loses energy, so more renewable capacity is needed upstream.
  6. Policy — so far focused mostly on road transport (FCEVs, refuelling stations).
"Technological or financial?" The technology largely exists, but it isn't ready for national networks, and the infrastructure is very expensive. So the barrier is mainly economic.

Hydrogen as a Fuel & Fuel Cells

Per kilogram, H2 is a superstar. Per cubic metre... not so much. And fuel cells dodge the Carnot limit.

Learning objectives: calculate HHV/LHV per kg and per m3 • compare Carnot vs fuel cell efficiency • use the Nernst equation • describe fuel cell losses.

Combustion Enthalpies

H2(g) + ½O2(g) → H2O(l)   ΔrH° = −285 kJ mol−1 (HHV)
H2(g) + ½O2(g) → H2O(g)   ΔrH° = −244 kJ mol−1 (LHV)
CH4(g) + 2O2(g) → CO2(g) + 2H2O(l)   ΔrH° = −890 kJ mol−1

HHV (Higher Heating Value): the water ends up as liquid, so the latent heat is recovered.
LHV (Lower Heating Value): the water stays as vapour. HHV − LHV ≈ ΔHvap(H2O) = 40.7 kJ mol−1.

Volumetric (LH2) = 142.5 × 70 ≈ 10 000 MJ m−3 (the lecture table gives ~8000)
CH4: 890 ÷ 16 = 55.6 MJ kg−1; × 0.65 = 36.3 MJ m−3

H2 has ~3× petrol's energy per kg, but ~1/3000 of it per m3 as a gas. That's why storage is the headache.

Heat Engines: the Carnot Limit

ηth = Wnet / Qin     ηCarnot = 1 − Tlow/Thigh

With Tflame = 1700 K and Tlow = 300 K: ηCarnot = 1 − 300/1700 = 0.82.
In practice: combined cycle plant ≤ 60%; diesel ≤ 35%; spark ignition ~20%.

Fuel Cell Thermodynamics

Anode: H2 → 2H+ + 2e−   E° = 0 V
Cathode: ½O2 + 2H+ + 2e− → H2O   E° = +1.23 V
ΔrG° = ΔrH° − TΔrS° = −285 + 48 = −237 kJ mol−1
ΔrG° = −nFE = −2 × 96485 × 1.23 = −237 kJ mol−1
ηFC,max = ΔrG°/ΔrH° = 237/285 = 0.83 (HHV) ≈ 0.94 (LHV)

This is the fuel cell's "analogue of Carnot". It isn't limited by temperature difference, because chemical energy goes straight to electrical work.

Nernst equation

Ecell = E°c − E°a + (RT/nF) ln[ pH2 · pO2½ / pH2O ]

Real Fuel Cell Losses

E = Ecell − b·log j − R·j − m·en·j
  • b·log j: activation loss (Tafel). Dominated by slow ORR (oxygen reduction) kinetics; HOR matters less.
  • R·j: ohmic loss from membrane ionic resistance (~1 A cm−2 region).
  • m·enj: mass transport limit at high load.

SECTION 1 — HYDROGEN ECONOMY

Hydrogen Safety & History

Why does H2 go bang? Branching chain reactions. That's the chemistry behind the famous disasters.

Learning objectives: compare unbranched vs branched chain reactions • quote flammability data • describe 3 disasters • explain hydrogen embrittlement • outline the history of H2/town gas.

Chain Reactions

Unbranched: H2 + Br2 (steady, no explosion)

Br2 → 2Br•  (initiation)
Br• + H2 → HBr + H•  (propagation)
H• + Br2 → HBr + Br•  (propagation)
Br• + Br• → Br2  (termination)

Applying the steady-state approximation to Br• and H•:

d[HBr]/dt = 2k2(k1/k4)½[H2][Br2]½

Branched: H2 + O2 (can explode!)

H2 + O2 → H• + HO2•  (initiation)
H• + O2 → O• + OH•  (branching: 1 radical → 2)
O• + H2 → OH• + H•  (branching)
OH• + H2 → H2O + H•  (propagation)
H• → wall; H• + O2 + M → HO2• + M; 2HO2• → H2O2 + O2  (termination)

d[H•]/dt = 2k2[H•][O2] − k6[H•][O2][M]

If branching beats termination, [H•] grows exponentially and you get an explosion.

Flammability Data (in air)

ParameterHydrogen
Lower flammability limit4 vol%
Upper flammability limit75 vol%
Minimum ignition energy0.016 mJ (a static spark is enough!)
Autoignition temperature500 °C
Wide flammability range + tiny ignition energy = the key safety issue. But H2 is very light and diffuses fast, so it disperses upwards quickly outdoors.

Notorious Disasters

  • Hindenburg (6 May 1937) — airship caught fire, probably from a static spark. The whole volume burned out within a few minutes.
  • Challenger (28 Jan 1986) — an O-ring leak; the shuttle broke up 73 s after lift-off, followed by LH2 explosion.
  • Fukushima (12 Mar 2011) — the tsunami knocked out the emergency generators, so the water pumps stopped and the cooling water overheated. Zr cladding reacted with steam (Zr + 2H2O → ZrO2 + 2H2), H2 built up and exploded, blowing the tops off the reactor buildings.

Hydrogen Embrittlement

This happens when all three are present:

  1. Diffusion of H atoms/ions into the metal
  2. Stress
  3. A susceptible metal (steel, Al, Ti, V)

Also, some metals (Ru, Pd) dissolve a lot of H2, which can cause leaks.

A Bit of History

  • Paracelsus (early 16th C) — noticed a gas when acid attacked metal.
  • Cavendish (1776) — studied "inflammable air".
  • Lavoisier (1783) — named it "hydro + gene" (= water-former); used H2 combustion to prove conservation of mass.

Coal gas / town gas

A by-product of coking (destructive distillation of coal). Coke (60% C) + steam → CO + H2. Used for lighting and cooking until the electricity grid took over.

1000 kg coal gives...Use
700 kg cokeIndustrial fuel
400 m3 coal gas (50% H2, 35% CH4, 10% CO, 5% C2H4)Fuel / light

50 L coal tarDyes, insecticides, medicines, yarn

Stored in gasometers (rigid waterless, or telescoping with water seals; the telescoping type was more common).

Practice Questions
  1. Explain, using the reaction mechanism, why H2/O2 mixtures can explode but H2/Br2 mixtures react steadily.
  2. State the flammability limits and minimum ignition energy of H2 in air.
  3. Explain the origin of the hydrogen in the Fukushima explosions.

One radical in, two radicals out — boom. Branching = exponential growth. Remember 4–75 vol% and 0.016 mJ.

Production Routes

Main industrial: SMR, methane pyrolysis, coal gasification, electrolysis.
Emerging: thermochemical cycles, photo(catalytic/microbial) water splitting, anaerobic biomass conversion.

SMR TRL 9

CH4 + H2O ⇌ CO + 3H2   ΔrH° = +206 kJ mol−1
800–1000 °C, 14–20 bar, Ni/Al2O3
Water–gas shift: CO + H2O ⇌ CO2 + H2   ΔrH° = −41 kJ mol−1
HTS 350 °C (Fe–Cr); LTS 250 °C (Cu–Zn/Al2O3)
Overall: CH4 + 2H2O → CO2 + 4H2   ΔrH° = +165 kJ mol−1
  • CH4 is a valuable, non-renewable resource.
  • The outlet is ~80% H2, so it needs purifying (PSA = pressure swing adsorption, or amine scrubbing).
  • Real plants emit 9–12 kg CO2 per kg H2.
  • Energy: the products hold more chemical energy than the CH4, because the endothermic heat is stored in the H2. In a closed system with reverse methanation, you could get zero carbon emission and zero carbon storage.

Coal Gasification TRL 9

Uses a sub-stoichiometric O2 supply (20–30%), with a combustion zone and an "anaerobic" zone.

Oxidation: C + ½O2 → CO (−111); CO + ½O2 → CO2 (−283); H2 + ½O2 → H2O (−242 kJ mol−1)
Gasification:
C + H2O ⇌ CO + H2  water–gas, +131
C + CO2 ⇌ 2CO  Boudouard, +172
C + 2H2 ⇌ CH4  methanation, −75 kJ mol−1

Used in IGCC (Integrated Gasification Combined Cycle). Messy, with lots of pollutants to capture, but useful for dealing with excess carbon.

Methane Pyrolysis TRL 3–6

CH4(g) → C(s) + 2H2(g)   ΔrH° = +75 kJ mol−1
  • Liquid bubble column of molten metals (Ti, Pb, Sn) or salts (KBr, NaF), with continuous carbon removal. Without a catalyst it needs 1100–1200 °C.
  • Energy: 890 kJ (burning CH4) vs 2 × 285 = 570 kJ (in the H2), so you sacrifice ~1/3 of the energy but emit no CO2.
  • Problems: separating the solid carbon, and stockpiling it.
Grey, blue, turquoise... it's all methane underneath. Grey = SMR, blue = SMR + CCS, turquoise = pyrolysis. Practise Problem 1 until you can do it in your sleep.

SECTION 2 — HYDROGEN PRODUCTION

Production 2: Electrolysis & Green Routes

Electrolysis is basically a fuel cell running backwards. You pay ΔG in electricity, and in practice a fair bit more.

Learning objectives: write acidic & alkaline electrolysis half-equations • define electrolyser efficiency • convert cell voltage into kWh/kg • describe thermochemical, photocatalytic and biological routes.

Water Electrolysis TRL 5–8

Acidic (PEM) electrolyte

Cathode: 2H+ + 2e− → H2   E° = 0 V
Anode: 2H2O → O2 + 4H+ + 4e−   E° = +1.23 V

Alkaline electrolyte (25–30% aq. KOH)

Cathode: 2H2O + 2e− → H2 + 2OH−   E° = −0.828 V
Anode: 2OH− → ½O2 + H2O + 2e−   E° = +0.401 V
Overall: H2O → H2 + ½O2   Ecell = −1.23 V

Anode: Ni-based catalyst (+Co, Mn). Cathode: Ni mesh.

Thermodynamics

ΔrG° = +237 kJ mol−1 = 285 − 48   (ΔG > 0, non-spontaneous)
ΔrG° = −nFEcell = −2 × 96485 × (−1.23) = +237 kJ mol−1
Ecell = E°c − E°a − (RT/nF) ln[pH2·pO2½/pH2O]
1.23 V = reversible voltage (pays ΔG only).
1.48 V = ΔH/2F = thermoneutral voltage (pays ΔG + TΔS as electricity). Real cells run at ~1.8–2.0 V.
ηel = energy content of products / total energy in = ΔrH° / (ΔrH° + 2Fηloss)
  • Losses = overvoltage + ohmic losses.
  • Faradaic efficiency < 1 because H2/O2 cross-permeate through the separator.
Worked Example: industrial kWh/kg at 2 V Charge per kg H2 = 500 mol × 2 × 96485 C = 9.65 × 107 C
Energy = QV = 9.65×107 × 2 = 1.93 × 108 J = 193 MJ
÷ 3.6 MJ/kWh ⇒ ≈ 53.6 kWh per kg H2
Per m3: 53.6 × 0.082 ≈ 4.4 kWh m−3 (STP)
Efficiency (HHV) = 1.48/2.0 = 39.4/53.6 ≈ 74%
Always state whether an efficiency is on an HHV or LHV basis. They differ by ~15%!

Thermochemical Cycles TRL 1–5

Direct thermolysis only gives 11% conversion even at 2500 °C. Cycles split water at lower T: all chemicals except water are recycled, and at least one step is a high-T endothermic one.

Iron–chloride cycle

3FeCl2 + 4H2O → Fe3O4 + 6HCl + H2  (650 °C)
Fe3O4 + 1.5Cl2 + 6HCl → 3FeCl3 + 3H2O + ½O2  (20 °C)
3FeCl3 → 3FeCl2 + 1.5Cl2  (420 °C)

Iodine–sulfur cycle

SO2 + I2 + 2H2O → H2SO4 + 2HI  (100 °C)
2HI → H2 + I2  (120–530 °C)
H2SO4 → H2O + SO2 + ½O2  (880 °C)

Photocatalytic Water Splitting TRL 1–4

Three steps: light absorption → charge separation → redox on co-catalysts. The competing problem is recombination of electrons and holes.

  • No good systems evolve H2 and O2 simultaneously.
  • Light delivery (illumination) is hard.
  • The best catalysts (e.g. Pt/TiO2) still need sacrificial hole scavengers.
  • Wide bandgaps mean most of the solar spectrum is wasted. Researchers are actively looking for two-photon (Z-scheme) systems.

Bio-H2 from Microalgae TRL 1–4

The idea is to modify photosynthesis into bio-photolysis. Efficiency and production rate are extremely low, so it needs huge illuminated areas or solar concentrators.

  1. Write both half-equations for alkaline electrolysis and calculate Ecell.
  2. An electrolyser runs at 1.9 V. Calculate the energy use in kWh/kg H2 and the HHV efficiency. (50.9 kWh/kg; 78%)
  3. Why is Faradaic efficiency less than 100%?

2F per H2. Always. Energy = 2F × V per mole. Remember 1.23 V (ΔG), 1.48 V (ΔH), and ~50–55 kWh/kg in practice.

Storing Hydrogen

0.082 kg m−3 is not much. You can squeeze it, freeze it, stick it to a surface, or bond it into a molecule.

Learning objectives: describe Type I–IV tanks • calculate compression work • explain liquefaction issues • compare hydrides, adsorbents and chemical carriers.

Four Storage Methods

  1. Compressed gas
  2. Liquid (cryogenic)
  3. Sorption (adsorption + absorption)
  4. Reversible hydrogenation (chemical carriers)

Judge them on: density, reversibility, delivery, storage losses, complexity.

Liquid Hydrogen (LH2)

  • The highest practical volumetric density: ~8 MJ L−1 (petrol is 32 MJ L−1).
  • −253 °C (20.3 K), plus ortho–para conversion (which releases heat and adds to boil-off).
  • Liquefied by compression → heat exchange → expansion (like a fridge).
  • H2 doesn't cool on throttling above its Joule–Thomson inversion temperature (−73 °C ≈ 200 K), so it must be pre-cooled (e.g. with LN2).
  • Energy-hungry: ~48 MJ kg−1 ≈ 40% of the H2 energy (LHV basis: 48/120).
  • Boil-off 1–4% per day. Example: the Suiso Frontier (Japan), with a 1250 m3 vacuum-insulated Type C tank.

Material-Based Storage

Metal hydrides (absorption), ΔH ~ 100 kJ mol−1

  • Elemental: M + x/2 H2 → MHx (Mg, Al, Na)
  • Intermetallic: AxBy + z/2 H2 → AxByHz (LaNi5, TiFe)
  • Complex: alanates [AlH4]−, borohydrides [BH4]−, amides [NH2]−
  • Bonded too strongly, so they need high T to release H2.

Physisorption, ΔH ~ 10 kJ mol−1

Langmuir:   a = amaxKadsPH2 / (1 + KadsPH2)
  • Bonded too weakly, so it needs 77 K (liquid N2).
  • High surface area (100+ m2 g−1), micropores 2–5 nm: porous carbon, MOFs, zeolites.
  • Best uptake 8–10 wt%.
  • Heat of adsorption: about 10 kg of N2 must evaporate per 1 kg of H2 adsorbed.
The "Goldilocks" target: a binding energy between 10 and 100 kJ mol−1. Not too weak, not too strong.

SECTION 4 — USE & TRANSPORT

Using & Transporting Hydrogen

Where does H2 actually make sense? Hard-to-electrify industry, seasonal storage, and some heavy transport.

Learning objectives: list H2 end uses by sector • explain hydrogen direct iron reduction • discuss solar/wind potential • compare electricity vs pipeline H2 transport.

Uses of Hydrogen

SectorExamples
IndustryPetrochemistry, fertiliser (NH3), steel, cement
TransportAviation, rail, shipping, cars, trucks, buses
HeatingIndustrial, domestic
Power sectorSeasonal energy storage, off-grid microgeneration, flexible conversion

Direct Iron Reduction (Green Steel)

Conventional (coal/coke): Fe3O4 + C → Fe + CO2, which means lots of CO2.
Hydrogen DRI: Fe3O4 + H2 → Fe + H2O, so the only by-product is water.
Steel refining: excess C in Fe is removed by C + O2 → CO2.

The lecture slides leave these unbalanced. If asked, balance them: Fe3O4 + 4H2 → 3Fe + 4H2O and Fe3O4 + 2C → 3Fe + 2CO2.

Self-Sustaining Household (Case Studies)

PV/wind → electrolyser → H2 store → fuel cell (+ battery for short-term). H2 covers seasonal mismatch (summer surplus → winter demand); batteries cover daily mismatch.

Socioeconomics

Solar and wind potential is very unevenly distributed (deserts for solar; coasts and high latitudes for wind). H2 could move energy from resource-rich to demand-rich regions, much like oil and LNG today.

Transport Options

  • Pipelines — gaseous H2. Could reuse the CH4 network, but watch out for embrittlement and H2's 3× lower volumetric energy.
  • Ships — LH2 (Suiso Frontier), NH3, methanol or LOHC.
  • Trucks — compressed tube trailers or LH2 tankers.
Exam-style: electricity vs pipeline H2, break-even distance Method:
1. H2 chain efficiency = ηelectrolysis × ηpumping × ηPEMFC
   e.g. 0.74 × 0.95 × 0.50 ≈ 0.35 (i.e. ~65% loss, roughly fixed)
2. Line loss grows with distance: ηline = (1 − f)L/1000 km
   e.g. HVDC f ≈ 3.5% per 1000 km
3. Break-even: (0.965)n = 0.35 ⇒ n = ln0.35/ln0.965 ≈ 30
   ⇒ ~30 000 km
Conclusion: wires win for almost any distance if the end use is electricity. H2 wins when the end use needs H2 (steel, NH3), when there's no grid, or for storage.
(Typical values I've assumed. Use your lecturer's numbers in the exam.)
Every conversion step costs energy (electricity → H2 → electricity loses ~65%), so don't convert unless you have to!
Practice Questions
  1. Give one use of H2 in each of industry, transport, heating and the power sector.
  2. Write balanced equations for the reduction of Fe3O4 by carbon and by hydrogen.
  3. Explain why hydrogen suits seasonal storage while batteries suit daily storage.
  4. Outline how you would estimate the distance above which pipeline H2 beats electricity transmission.
Hydrogen: don't use it where a wire will do. Best uses: steel, ammonia, refining, long-duration storage and heavy transport.

SECTION 5 — BATTERIES

Batteries & the Li-ion Cell

A battery turns chemical free energy into the work of electrons. Learn the general A + C ⇌ B + D picture, then apply it to Li-ion.

Learning objectives: link ΔG to cell voltage • use the Nernst and Butler–Volmer equations • calculate specific capacity (mAh g−1) • describe operating windows & degradation • describe lead–acid and flow batteries.

Working Principle

Negative: A → B + ne−    Positive: C + ne− → D
Overall: A + C ⇌ B + D   (discharge → / ← charge)
ΔG = −nFΔE

ΔG < 0: spontaneous (discharge). ΔG = 0: equilibrium (flat battery). ΔG > 0: needs external power (charge).

Voltage vs state of charge (x)

ΔEcell = ΔE°cell − (RT/nF) ln[ x·x / (1−x)(1−x) ]

This gives a gentle S-shaped curve: a flat plateau in the middle, with steep drops at full and empty.

Butler–Volmer (kinetics)

j = j0[ (COx(0,t)/C*Ox) eαazFη/RT − (CRed(0,t)/C*Red) e−αczFη/RT ]

η = overpotential (E − E°). A high exchange current j0 means only a small overpotential is needed, so the electrode is fast. The terminal voltage is lower than the OCV on discharge and higher on charge.

Lithium-ion Cell

Negative: LixC6 → C6 + xLi+ + xe−   (0.25 V vs Li+/Li)
Positive: Li1−xCoO2 + xLi+ + xe− → LiCoO2   (4.2 V vs Li+/Li)
Cell: 2LiCoO2 + 6C ⇌ LiC6 + 2Li0.5CoO2

Li+ shuttles between the two hosts (the "rocking chair").

Specific capacity (mAh g−1) = x·F / (3.6 × M)   (1 mAh = 3.6 C)
Q1: graphite (x = 1) M(C6) = 72 g mol−1
Q = 96485/(3.6 × 72) = 372 mAh g−1
(If you divide by the mass of LiC6, 78.9 g mol−1, you get 339 mAh g−1. 372 is the standard value.)
Q2: LiCoO2 (x = 0.5) M = 6.94 + 58.93 + 2(16) = 97.87 g mol−1
Q = 0.5 × 96485/(3.6 × 97.87) = 137 mAh g−1
Q3: full cell Vmax = 4.2 − 0.25 = 3.95 V
Combined: 1/Q = 1/372 + 1/137 ⇒ Q ≈ 100 mAh g−1
(Check: 26 801 mAh / (2×97.87 + 72) g = 100 mAh g−1)
Specific energy ≈ 3.95 × 100 ≈ 395 Wh kg−1 (active materials only)
Combine capacities like resistors in parallel (reciprocals add). The weaker electrode, here LiCoO2, dominates.

Rate & Temperature Effects

A Yuasa cell kept 88% of its capacity at 300 A and 86% at −25 °C. Higher current or lower temperature means more overpotential, so the cell hits cut-off voltage sooner.

Moderate energy capacity but good power capability.

The electrolytes (e.g. vanadium in VRFB) are stored in external tanks and pumped through the cell stack, so energy (tank size) and power (stack size) scale independently. Good for grid storage.

SECTION 5 — BATTERIES

Capacitors & Supercapacitors

Batteries store charge chemically, capacitors store it electrostatically. One gives energy, the other gives power.

Learning objectives: use Q = CV, C = ε0εrA/d, E = ½CV², Pmax = V²/4ESR • contrast batteries vs capacitors • describe EDLC and pseudocapacitors • read a Ragone-type plot.

Capacitor Basics

Q = CV     C = ε0εrA/d
E = ½CV²     Pmax = V²/(4·ESR)

ESR = equivalent series resistance. Mind the breakdown voltage: E ∝ V², so higher voltage helps a lot, but only up to breakdown.

Capacitor vs Battery

CapacitorBattery
Physical (electrostatic) storageChemical (Faradaic) storage
High powerLower power, higher energy
Linear V vs chargePlateau V vs charge
Very long cycle lifeLimited cycle life

Electrical Double-Layer Capacitor (EDLC)

Two porous carbon electrodes, each forming a double layer with the electrolyte. They act as two capacitors in series:

1/Ccell = 1/C+ + 1/C−   ⇒   Ccell = Ce/2 (when C+ = C−)
Ce (F g−1) = C (µF cm−2) × A (m2 g−1) × 10−2    with C ≈ 10–30 µF cm−2
Worked Example Carbon with A = 1000 m2 g−1 and C = 20 µF cm−2:
Ce = 20 × 1000 × 10−2 = 200 F g−1, so Ccell = 100 F per g of one electrode.
At 2.7 V: E = ½ × 100 × 2.7² ≈ 365 J ≈ 0.10 Wh per gram

ESR depends on:

  • choice of active electrode material and its resistance
  • the electrolyte and its ionic resistance
  • interfacial resistance between electrode and current collector
  • ionic (diffusion) resistance in small pores and the separator

Pseudocapacitors

Thin layers or nanostructures of a redox-active phase (e.g. RuO2, MnO2) store extra charge through fast surface Faradaic reactions:

A ⇌ B + ze−    Q = znF    ΔG° = −znFE°

n = moles of redox phase. This gives more energy than an EDLC and keeps most of its power. It sits between capacitor and battery.

SECTION 6 — PHOTOVOLTAICS

Silicon Photovoltaics

Silicon dominates PV. Know how the p–n junction works, how to read an I–V curve, and how a cell is made.

Learning objectives: explain carriers & the p–n junction • use the Shockley equation, Voc, Isc, FF, η • describe Si manufacture • calculate IPCE and LHE (Beer–Lambert).

Semiconductor Basics

  • Photons with hν ≥ Eg create electron–hole pairs.
  • n-type (P-doped): electrons are the majority carriers. p-type (B-doped): holes are the majority.
  • At the p–n junction, the space charge region (SCR) holds a built-in field that separates the carriers. Minority carriers must reach the SCR before they recombine.

The I–V Curve

Shockley:   I = IL − I0[exp(qV/nkT) − 1]
Voc = (nkT/q) ln(IL/I0 + 1)    Isc ≈ IL
FF = Pmax/(IscVoc)    η = FF·Isc·Voc/Pin

The fill factor (FF, called "form factor" in the slides) measures how "square" the I–V curve is (~0.8 for good cells). Pin for AM1.5 = 1000 W m−2.

Single-junction efficiency is capped by the Shockley–Queisser (SQ) limit, ~33% at Eg ≈ 1.3–1.4 eV. Photons below Eg aren't absorbed, and photons above Eg lose their excess energy as heat (thermalisation).

Making a Si Module

  1. Silicon production & purification: quartz → metallurgical Si → purified (e.g. Siemens process) → polysilicon.
  2. Wafers: single-crystal (Czochralski ingot, more efficient) or polycrystalline (cast, cheaper).
  3. Surface texturing (pyramids) to reduce reflection.
  4. Phosphorus doping (diffusion) to form the n+ emitter on the p-wafer.
  5. Antireflection coating (e.g. SiNx).
  6. Screen-printed contacts (Ag front grid, Al back).
  7. Module: cells in series, with EVA encapsulant, glass front, backsheet and frame.

Measuring Performance: IPCE

IPCE(%) = 1240 [eV nm] × Jsc [µA cm−2] / ( λ [nm] × Φ [µW cm−2] )
IPCE = LHE × φinj × ηc

LHE = light harvesting efficiency; φinj = electron injection quantum yield; ηc = current collection efficiency.

Beer–Lambert Law

I = I0e−αL    α = −(2.303/L) log(I/I0)
A = log(I0/I) = εcl    LHE = 1 − I/I0 = 1 − 10−A = 1 − e−αL

Units: α in cm−1; ε in dm3 mol−1 cm−1; c in mol dm−3; l in cm.

Worked Example: IPCE At λ = 500 nm, Φ = 100 µW cm−2, Jsc = 30 µA cm−2:
IPCE = 1240 × 30/(500 × 100) = 0.744 ⇒ 74.4%
Don't confuse log10 (absorbance A) with ln (α). They differ by the factor 2.303.

SECTION 6 — PHOTOVOLTAICS

Thin Film (CIGS) & Multijunction Cells

To "boldly go beyond the SQ limit", stack several semiconductors so each one gets the part of the spectrum it suits best.

Learning objectives: describe CIGS structure, manufacture and issues • explain spectrum splitting & current matching • choose band gaps from a cumulative current table • size layer thicknesses.

CIGS: Cu(In,Ga)Se2

A chalcopyrite family (CuFeS2 structure, tetragonal, I–III–VI2), e.g. roquesite CuInS2 and gallite CuGaS2.

Stack: soda-lime glass / Mo (sputtered) / p-CIGS (co-evaporated or selenised) / n-CdS (chemical bath) / n+ ZnO TCO (magnetron sputtering or CVD) / metal grid.

  • Composition is flexible (In, Ga, S, Se). The band gap is tuned with Ga, x = Ga/(Ga+In), typically 1.1–1.2 eV.
  • Grain size is controlled by Cu, but it has little effect on efficiency.
  • A forgiving material: tolerates impurities, has passive grain boundaries, and is insensitive to the quality of the CdS/CIGS interface.
  • Type inversion near the interface (ΔEc ≈ 0.5 V) puts the maximum recombination in the depletion region, not at the interface.

Concerns: Cd toxicity • high-vacuum processing • In and Ga are rare and costly • CdS absorbs hν > 2.54 eV (parasitic loss).
Records: cell 22.9% (1 cm2); module 15.7% (600 cm2).

Multijunction (Tandem) Cells

  • Semiconductors split the spectrum: the widest-gap layer faces the light, with narrower gaps underneath.
  • The subcells are diodes in series, so current matching is required (Icell = smallest subcell current).
  • Vtotal ≈ Eg1 + Eg2 + ... (theoretical maximum)

Choosing Band Gaps (Cumulative Current Method)

The table gives the cumulative AM1.5 photocurrent available for all photons with E ≥ Eg. Each subcell's current = cum(Eg,i) − cum(Eg,i−1). For matching, set every subcell to Itotal/N.

5-junction example (bottom gap 0.77 eV, cum = 567 A m−2) I per subcell = 567/5 ≈ 113 A m−2. Read off where cum = 113, 227, 340, 453, 567:
Eg ≈ 2.20, 1.70, 1.37, 1.07, 0.77 eV
Vmax ≈ Σ Eg = 7.11 V
P = 7.11 × 113 ≈ 806 W m−2 ⇒ ηmax ≈ 80% (ideal, FF = 1)
With FF = 0.8: ≈ 645 W m−2 ⇒ ~64%
Layer thickness for LHE = 99% 1 − e−αL = 0.99 ⇒ L = ln(100)/α = 4.605/α
α = 3, 8, 5, 4, 1 µm−1 ⇒ L = 1.54, 0.58, 0.92, 1.15, 4.61 µm
Cs3Bi2Br9−xIx perovskite 3-junction Best: Cs3Bi2Br9 (2.66 eV, 44) / Cs3Bi2Br6I3 (2.24 eV, 98 − 44 = 54) / Cs3Bi2I9 (1.99 eV, 148 − 98 = 50)
Matched I = 44 A m−2; V = 6.89 V
P = 6.89 × 44 × 0.8 = 242.5 W m−2 ⇒ η ≈ 24%
Alternative (Br5I4, 2.19 eV): I = 40 A m−2, P = 218.9 W m−2, which is worse.
The top cell's current sets the ceiling. Choose the middle gap so the lower cells can match it but not starve the bottom cell.

Real Devices

GaInP / GaAs / Ge (1.91 / 1.42 / 0.8 eV): monolithic, splits the spectrum, minimal thermalisation, very high efficiency. Downsides: sensitive to spectral changes (time of day, clouds) and expensive, so mainly used in space and concentrator PV.
a-Si/Ge triple junction: a cheaper thin-film tandem.

Series = same current. Pick band gaps that split the cumulative current equally. V ≈ ΣEg, η = V·I·FF/1000.
