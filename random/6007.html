Section 1 — Light & the Solar Spectrum

Solar Radiation, Air Mass & Irradiance

Before you can turn sunlight into electricity, you need to know how much sunlight there is. It's all about getting the quantities (and their units) straight.

1Above vs. at the ground

  • AM0 — outside the atmosphere. Solar constant S ≈ 1366 W m⁻² (estimated from blackbody theory, Planck's law).
  • At the ground you get direct + diffuse + reflected radiation. The atmosphere absorbs certain wavelengths (O₃ in the UV; H₂O and CO₂ in the IR).
TermMeaning
GHIGlobal Horizontal — direct on a horizontal plane + diffuse sky
DHIDiffuse Horizontal — diffuse sky only, horizontal plane
DNIDirect Normal — direct beam on a surface ⟂ to the Sun

2Air Mass = path-length ratio

Learn it
AM ≈ Lθz / Lz = 1 / cos θz
θz = zenith angle. AM1.5 ⇒ θz ≈ 48.2° (mid-latitude Sun).
AM1.5G ≈ 1.0 kW m⁻² — the reference spectrum (ASTM/IEC) used to calibrate and compare solar cells at STC.
Lower Sun → longer path → more absorption & scattering.

3Irradiance vs. irradiation

Don't mix these up — one is power, one is energy.

Learn it

Irradiance (power per unit area):

G = P / A   [W m⁻²]

Irradiation (energy per unit area over time):

H = ∫ G(t) dt   [Wh m⁻²]
Irradiance–time curve: the area underneath is the irradiation.
  • Irradiance (standard form AM1.5G) → characterises & calibrates cells.
  • Irradiation → describes climate; used as the energy input in system design.
Unit trick: 1 kW m⁻² = 100 mW cm⁻². (1 m² = 10⁴ cm².) You'll use this all the time.

4Light is a wave AND a particle

ModelExplainsUsed in PV for…
Particle (photon)Discrete energy quanta; absorption is countablePhoton energy, carrier generation, current
WaveInterference, diffraction, reflection, refractionAntireflection coatings, concentrators, light-trapping
Practice Questions
  1. Calculate the air mass when the Sun is at a zenith angle of 60°.
  2. A day's irradiance curve has an area of 5.2 kWh m⁻². Is this irradiance or irradiation? Explain.
Exam Tip: If the units are W m⁻², it's irradiance (a rate). If they're Wh m⁻², it's irradiation (an amount). Examiners love catching people who mix them up.

Photon Energy & Photon Flux

A solar cell doesn't care about watts. It cares about how many photons arrive (→ current) and how much energy each one carries (→ voltage).

1Einstein's relation

Learn it
Eph = hν = hc / λ
h = 6.626×10⁻³⁴ J s · c = 2.998×10⁸ m s⁻¹ · λ in metres

In electronvolts (1 eV = 1.602×10⁻¹⁹ J):

E (eV) = 1.240 / λ(µm) = 1240 / λ(nm)
  • Longer wavelength → lower photon energy. E.g. 1 eV ↔ 1240 nm.
  • dE/dλ = −hc/λ² = −E/λ (the minus sign means "longer λ, less energy").
WORKED EXAMPLE — a 300 nm photon
  1. λ = 300×10⁻⁹ m
  2. E = (6.626×10⁻³⁴)(2.998×10⁸)/(300×10⁻⁹) = 6.62×10⁻¹⁹ J
  3. E = 6.62×10⁻¹⁹ / 1.602×10⁻¹⁹ = 4.13 eV  (check: 1240/300 = 4.13 ✓)

2Power → photons

For a monochromatic beam of power P on area A, divide by the energy per photon:

Learn the chain
QuantityFormulaUnit
Irradiance GP / AW m⁻²
Photon flux ΨP / Eph = Pλ/hcs⁻¹
Photon-flux density ΦΨ / A = G / Eph = Gλ/hcm⁻² s⁻¹
WORKED EXAMPLE — Quiz 1 (a favourite!)
G = 1000 W m⁻², λ = 500 nm, A = 1 cm². Find P, Eph, Ψ, Φ.
  1. P = GA = 1000 × 10⁻⁴ = 0.1 W (= 100 mW)
  2. Eph = hc/λ = 3.98×10⁻¹⁹ J = 1240/500 = 2.48 eV
  3. Φ = G/Eph = 1000 / (2.48×1.602×10⁻¹⁹) = 2.52×10²¹ m⁻² s⁻¹
  4. Ψ = AΦ = 10⁻⁴ × 2.52×10²¹ = 2.52×10¹⁷ s⁻¹
Why eV? Numbers are friendly (≈1–4 eV), and E in eV ≈ max voltage in V.

3The units correspondence table

Radiant (power) sidePhoton side
Power P [W = J s⁻¹]Photon flux Ψ [s⁻¹]
Irradiance G [W m⁻²]Photon-flux density Φ [s⁻¹ m⁻²]
Spectral irradiance eλ [W m⁻² µm⁻¹] or eE [W m⁻² eV⁻¹]Spectral photon-flux density φλ [s⁻¹ m⁻² µm⁻¹] or φE [s⁻¹ m⁻² eV⁻¹]
Going across the table = divide by Eph (in J!). Going down = divide by area, then by bandwidth.
Practice Questions
  1. Find the energy (J and eV) of a 1100 nm photon.
  2. A 5 mW laser at 650 nm hits 1 mm². Find Ψ and Φ.
Exam Tip: When dividing W by photon energy, the energy MUST be in joules. Using eV gives you an answer that's out by a factor of 1.6×10⁻¹⁹ — a classic dropped mark.

Spectral Irradiance & Integrating Spectra

Sunlight isn't one wavelength, it's a whole spread. "Spectral" just means "per unit wavelength" (or per unit photon energy).

Area under the AM1.5G eλ curve = GSTC ≈ 1000 W m⁻². Dips = molecular absorption.
Learn it — integrate to get totals
G = ∫λ₁λ₂ eλ dλ     Φ = ∫λ₁λ₂ φλ dλ
φλ = eλ / Eph = λ eλ / hc
Whole spectrum: limits 0 → ∞. Analytic for neat functions; numerical (sum of bins) for measured data.

1Three kinds of light

TypeShapeIrradiance
MonochromaticVertical line, zero width (ideal)Gml = eλ numerically (different units!)
Quasi-mono (laser/LED)Narrow band, finite width δλGqml = eλδλ
Broadband (Sun)Continuous spectrumG = ∫eλdλ

2Narrow bands: rectangles & trapezoids

Gbin = ½(eλ1 + eλ2)(λ₂ − λ₁)
Φbin = ½(eλ1/Eλ1 + eλ2/Eλ2)(λ₂ − λ₁)
Classic mistake: summing the eλ column (or λ·eλ) and calling it G. Wrong! eλ is per µm — every bin must be × Δλ. Unit check: (W m⁻² µm⁻¹)(µm) = W m⁻² ✓

3Changing the axis: λ → E (the Jacobian)

Same beam, same total G, so the areas must match: eE dE = eλ dλ.

Learn it
|dλ/dE| = λ / E   ⇒   eE = eλ λ / E(eV) = eλ λ² / 1.24
φE = eE / (qE)    [s⁻¹ m⁻² eV⁻¹]
  • On the E-axis the UV moves to the right and the IR to the left; peak widths change.
  • You can't plot φλ against E. It's normalised per µm, not per eV.
WORKED EXAMPLE — Quiz 2 (500 nm, eλ = 1550.3 W m⁻² µm⁻¹)
  • δλ = 5 nm: G = 1550.3 × 0.005 = 7.75 W m⁻²
  • Φ = 7.75 / 3.97×10⁻¹⁹ = 1.95×10¹⁹ m⁻² s⁻¹
  • eE = 1550.3×0.5/2.48 = 312.6 W m⁻² eV⁻¹
  • δE = 1 meV: G = 312.6×10⁻³ = 0.313 W m⁻²; Φ = 0.313/(2.48×1.602×10⁻¹⁹) = 7.87×10¹⁷ m⁻² s⁻¹
Summary: spectral quantity × bandwidth = ordinary quantity. Changing the axis? Multiply by the Jacobian |dλ/dE| = λ/E so that the total area (G) is conserved.

Band Theory & the Efficiency Limit

Semiconductors have a bandgap Eg, an energy "no-go zone" between the filled valence band and the empty conduction band. It decides which photons get absorbed and how much energy you keep.

1Metals, semiconductors, insulators

Atomic orbitals combine (LCAO) into bands. Si: 1s²2s²2p⁶3s²3p², so 4 valence electrons give 4 covalent bonds in a diamond (tetrahedral) lattice.

2Absorption & thermalisation

  • Photon with hν ≥ Eg → creates an electron–hole pair. Photons with hν < Eg are not absorbed.
  • The excess energy (hν − Eg) is lost as heat as carriers relax to the band edges. This is thermalisation, and it's unavoidable.
  • Then the e–h pair can recombine (light or heat). That's reducible, but never zero.

3Maximum conversion efficiency

Thick cell, no reflection. Each absorbed photon gives one pair worth Eg:

Learn it
Gabs = ∫Eg∞ E φE dE    Gextr = Eg∫Eg∞ φE dE
η = Eg∫Eg∞φEdE / ∫0∞EφEdE
Includes below-gap loss + thermalisation. Does not include recombination.
Upper curve: photon-energy limit. Lower curve: Shockley–Queisser (adds radiative recombination, FF<1).
Why a middle Eg wins: a small Eg absorbs lots of photons but keeps little energy per photon. A large Eg keeps lots per photon but absorbs few. ~1.1–1.4 eV is the sweet spot.

4Direct vs indirect gaps

MaterialEg (eV)Type
c-Si1.12indirect → thick (~200–500 µm)
a-Si~1.75direct
CuInSe₂ / CdTe / GaAs / InP1.05 / 1.45 / 1.42 / 1.34direct → thin films

An indirect transition needs a phonon (lattice vibration) as well as the photon, so absorption is weaker and the layer has to be thicker. Direct-gap materials absorb strongly, which is why satellites like thin films (better power-to-weight).

Summary: Eg sets which photons are absorbed (hν ≥ Eg) and the energy kept per photon (Eg). Thermalisation and below-gap transmission are the fundamental losses.

Doping, the PN Junction & the Diode Equation

A solar cell is basically a big illuminated diode. Doping creates the junction, and the junction's built-in field separates the electrons and holes.

1Doping

TypeDopantEffect
Intrinsicnone (pure)High resistance; EF mid-gap
N-typeGroup V donor (P, As)Extra free electrons; EF near CB
P-typeGroup III acceptor (B, Al)Missing bond electrons = holes; EF near VB

Light (non-degenerate) doping doesn't change Eg. Si unit cell = 2 atoms; conventional (FCC) cell = 8 atoms (8×⅛ + 6×½ + 4 inside).

2Forming the junction

Electrons diffuse N→P until EF is equal. P side bands go up, N side bands go down.
  • The depletion region is an ultra-thin layer of charged ions with a built-in field. Carriers get swept across very fast (little chance to recombine).
  • qVbi = EFn − EFp = ΔCBM = ΔVBM, and Vbi ≤ Eg.

3Currents are thermally activated (Arrhenius)

Learn it
Rate = ω exp(−E / kBT)
kB = 1.381×10⁻²³ J K⁻¹ = 8.617×10⁻⁵ eV K⁻¹ ; kBT ≈ 25 meV at 300 K (thermal voltage kBT/q ≈ 25.7 mV)

4Three scenarios → three equations

A) Equilibrium (dark, no bias)
I₁ (N→P, climbing Vbi) = C₁e−qVbi/kT;  I₂ (P→N, across Eg) = C₂e−Eg/kT
I₁ = I₂ ⇒ net I = 0. Define the dark saturation current I₀ = I₁ = I₂ (small).

B) Applied bias V, dark. The barrier drops to Vbi − V, so I₁ = I₀eqV/kT while I₂ = I₀:

Shockley diode equation
I = I₀ [exp(qV / kBT) − 1]

C) Bias + light. Add the photocurrent Il (electron-flow sign):

I = I₀[exp(qV/kBT) − 1] − Il
Find I₀ experimentally: measure dark I–V, then plot ln I vs V. The slope is q/kBT and the intercept gives ln I₀.
Practice Questions
  1. Why does a diode conduct in one direction only? Use the diode equation.
  2. Sketch the band diagram of a PN junction and label EF, Vbi and the depletion region.
Exam Tip: In the derivation, only the N→P current (I₁) depends on V, because bias changes the barrier height Vbi. The P→N current (I₂ = I₀) depends only on Eg.

The Ideal Solar Cell: I–V Curve & G, T Effects

Flip the 4th-quadrant curve into the 1st quadrant (electrical current runs opposite to electron flow) and you get the famous solar-cell I–V curve.

Learn it — ideal cell
I = Il − I₀[exp(qV/kBT) − 1]
ISC = Il  (V = 0)
VOC = (kBT/q) ln(Il/I₀ + 1) ≈ (kBT/q) ln(Il/I₀)
Pmax = ImVm = FF·ISCVOC    η = Pmax/Pin
FF = (green box area)/(grey box area) = ImVm/(ISCVOC) < 1 because of the exponential.

7 characteristic parameters: ISC, VOC, Im, Vm, Pmax, FF, η.

1General principles

  • Two steps: (i) absorb light → mobile carriers; (ii) separate charge → electricity.
  • At most 1 electron per photon: Jmax ≤ qΦ → current follows photon flux.
  • qV ≤ Eg → voltage is bounded by photon energy (an upper bound, not a guarantee).
  • Jl = qΦ × QE. EQE = collected electrons ÷ incident photons; IQE = ÷ absorbed photons.

2Irradiance G

  • ISC ∝ G (linear): Il(G) ≈ (G/GSTC) Il,STC
  • VOC rises logarithmically: ΔVOC = (kBT/q) ln(G/GSTC)

3Temperature T

  • I₀ = C exp(−Eg/kBT) rises steeply with T, so VOC falls almost linearly.
  • ISC barely changes with T.
Learn it — the big VOC equation
VOC = VOC,STC + (kBT/q) ln(G/GSTC) − β(T − TSTC)
β = (Eg/q − VOC,STC) / TSTC
STC: 1 kW m⁻², AM1.5G, 25 °C = 298.15 K. T in kelvin. Use G/GSTC (or G in kW m⁻²) inside the log. β is given by manufacturers.
WORKED EXAMPLE — IL,ref = 4.00 A, VOC,ref = 0.650 V, β = 2.00 mV K⁻¹

(a) 500 W m⁻², 25 °C: IL = 4.00×0.5 = 2.00 A
kBT/q = 8.617×10⁻⁵×298.15 = 0.02569 V
VOC = 0.650 + 0.02569 ln 0.5 = 0.632 V

(b) 500 W m⁻², 50 °C (T = 323.15 K, ΔT = 25 K):
log term = 0.02785 ln 0.5 = −0.0193 V; temp term = −0.002×25 = −0.050 V
VOC = 0.650 − 0.0193 − 0.050 = 0.581 V

Irradiance effect −19.3 mV; temperature effect −50 mV. Heat hurts far more than dimming!

Doubling G doubles ISC but only adds (kBT/q)ln 2 ≈ 18 mV to VOC. At open circuit photogeneration hasn't stopped. The diode current just balances Il.
Practice Questions
  1. Why does a cell deliver zero power at both short circuit and open circuit?
  2. A cell has Il = 35 mA and I₀ = 10⁻¹² A at 300 K. Find VOC.

Series & Shunt Resistance

Real cells aren't ideal. Rs (contacts, wires, bulk) and Rsh (leakage paths) both squash the fill factor and steal power.

Full equivalent circuit: photocurrent source ∥ diode ∥ shunt, then Rs in series.
Learn it — combined model
I = IL − I₀[exp(q(V+IRs)/nkBT) − 1] − (V+IRs)/Rsh
n = ideality factor (≈1 for most commercial cells; take n = 1 unless told). The equation is implicit (I is on both sides).

1Series resistance Rs

  • Drops voltage IRs → rounds the knee and tilts the curve near VOC. Dissipates I²Rs.
  • Little effect on VOC (I = 0) and on ISC (unless huge). Mainly hits FF.
Learn it
rs = Rs ISC/VOC = Rs/RCH    FF ≈ FF₀(1 − rs)
Pmax ≈ ISCVOCFF₀(1 − rs)
RCH = VOC/ISC is the characteristic resistance. Use Ag contacts → small Rs.

2Shunt resistance Rsh

  • A leakage path (e.g. the base touching the top electrode). It diverts Ish = VD/Rsh away from the load.
  • A low Rsh gives a steep slope near ISC and lowers FF (and VOC if very low).
  • Want Rsh high (≳10³ × Rs). It matters more for thin films than for c-Si.
Diagnosing from curve shape. A shape suggests a cause but doesn't prove it. Check with dark I–V and contact tests.
WORKED EXAMPLE — FF loss from Rs
ISC = 5.00 A, VOC = 0.650 V, FF₀ = 0.800, Rs = 0.0200 Ω
  1. Pmax,0 = 0.8×5×0.65 = 2.60 W
  2. rs = 0.02×5/0.65 = 0.154
  3. FF ≈ 0.8(1−0.154) = 0.677 → Pmax ≈ 2.20 W
  4. Loss = 0.40 W = 15.4% (approx. A full I–V solve finds the true MPP.)
WORKED EXAMPLE — combined model at one point
IL = 1 A, I₀ = 4.733×10⁻⁸ A, n = 1.5, T = 298.15 K, Rs = 0.08 Ω, Rsh = 3 Ω, VD = 0.560 V
  • nkBT/q = 0.03854 V → ID = 4.733×10⁻⁸(e14.53−1) ≈ 0.0968 A
  • Ish = VD/Rsh = 0.560/3 = 0.1867 A
  • I = 1 − 0.0968 − 0.1867 = 0.7165 A; V = 0.560 − 0.7165×0.08 = 0.5027 V
  • P = VI ≈ 0.360 W; I* = I/IL = 0.717
The shunt sees VD, not V. Using V/Rsh underestimates leakage.
Solving the implicit equation: sweep the internal voltage V' = V + IRs, calculate I from the ideal equation, then get V = V' − IRs. Easy in Excel/Python!

Normalised current I* = I/IL (dimensionless). Use the same IL for all curves, otherwise you hide the current losses.

Energy Losses in Solar Cells

Where does 100 mW cm⁻² of sunlight go? Sort every loss into three bins: fundamental, optical and collection.

Photons→absorption & thermalisation→e–h pairs (minority carriers)→transport to junction→separation→majority carriers to contacts→Electricity

1Fundamental (unavoidable)

  • No below-gap absorption (E < Eg → no current)
  • Thermalisation: excess (E − Eg) lost as heat
  • Fill factor < 1: the exponential I–V curve isn't rectangular

2Optical losses

  1. Top-surface reflection (significant). Fix with an ARC + texturing.
  2. Top-contact shading by fingers/bus bars.
  3. Incomplete absorption (transmission). Fix with thickness and light trapping.
Learn it
Jl = qΦ·EQE = qΦ(1 − R − T)·IQE
IQE = EQE/(1 − R − T), assuming no parasitic absorption. EQE is measured with calibrated monochromatic light vs λ.

3Antireflection coatings (quarter-wave)

Make the two reflected beams cancel (destructive interference, phase difference π). Extra path 2d = λARC/2:

Learn it
d = λair / (4 nARC)
e.g. λ = 600 nm, n = 2.0 → d = 75 nm. (nARC = λair/λARC)

Why are ARC-coated Si cells blue? The ARC is tuned for mid-visible light, so the leftover reflection is mainly blue (≈400–450 nm). ARC + texturing reflects weak blue + red, which looks grey.

4Collection losses

  • Recombination: band-to-band (radiative or non-radiative), and defect-assisted in the bulk and at surfaces. Defect-assisted recombination is the main source; it reduces VOC and JSC.
  • Series resistance (ohmic losses to the contacts): Pmax ≈ ISCVOCFF₀(1 − Rs/RCH)
  • Shunt: minor for c-Si, significant for thin films.

5The c-Si loss "waterfall"

6Two efficiency limits

Photon-energy limitShockley–Queisser
Peak49% @ 1.12 eV33.7% @ 1.34 eV
KeepsqEg per photon, FF = 1, VOC = Eg, no recombinationRadiative recombination only, n = 1, 300 K, front emission only, VOC < Eg, FF < 1
"Fundamental" = physics you can't beat with one junction. "Optical" and "collection" = engineering you can improve.

The Four Families of Solar Cells

Wafers, thin films, epitaxial III–Vs and molecular/emerging cells. Know the structure, the main absorber and how the carriers move.

1Crystalline silicon

  • Indirect gap → thick (200–500 µm). Most current is generated in the p-base, far from the junction field, so transport there is mainly by diffusion.
  • Single-junction record designs: HIT/HJT, TOPCon.

2Thin films (a-Si, CdTe, CIGS)

  • a-Si: direct gap → hundreds of nm thick. A p⁺–i–n⁺ structure: heavy doping gives a built-in field across the thin absorber, so drift current dominates.
  • CdS buffer in CdTe/CIGS: (1) passivates the absorber surface, (2) antireflection, (3) blocks holes from going back for recombination.
  • CIGS: ZnO / CdS / Cu(In,Ga)Se₂ / Mo / glass. Lots of interfaces. Made by CVD/PVD.
  • Why bother? Far less semiconductor → cheaper large-scale PV.

3GaAs (III–V)

  • Absorbs in the p-side, with both diffusion and drift currents.
  • A p-GaAlAs window blocks electrons from exiting at the top electrode and passivates the surface (less recombination).

4Molecular cells

  • Dye-sensitised (DSSC): dye (e.g. N719) absorbs → electron injected into the CB of mesoporous TiO₂ (high surface area = more dye). An electrolyte carries holes to the counter electrode. Problems: liquid leakage, dye photodegradation. Alternatives to dye: quantum dots, perovskite.
  • Organic: donor + acceptor blend → bulk heterojunction. Holes travel in the donor, electrons in the acceptor. Interfaces are crucial for charge separation.

5Cost per watt chart

Learn it
$/W = cost per m² / (η × 1000 W m⁻²)
x-axis: cost/m²; y-axis: η. Dashed lines = constant $/W. A cheaper panel isn't necessarily cheaper per watt!
Thermodynamic limit > SQ limit. Multijunctions dodge the SQ single-gap losses, so they're the "bright future" for high efficiency.

Multijunction (Tandem) Solar Cells

One bandgap wastes photons. So stack several: each subcell converts the slice of the spectrum it's best at, which cuts thermalisation. That's how MJ cells beat the single-junction SQ limit.

1Why the big gap goes on top

If the small-gap cell were on top, it would absorb everything and thermalise the high-energy photons. With the big gap on top, the blue photons are converted at a high voltage and the red ones pass through to the lower cell.

2Tunnel junction

  • An ultra-thin, heavily doped p⁺⁺/n⁺⁺ layer (or ultrathin insulator) that electrons tunnel through. T ∝ exp(−2κd), so a thicker barrier means less tunnelling.
  • Roles: connects subcells in series (same current, voltages add); acts as a recombination contact (electrons from one cell meet holes from the other); low voltage drop; transparent to the light going down.
Vtandem = Vtop + Vbottom − ΔVint    Ploss/A = JΔVint

3Current matching (2-terminal)

In series, the same current flows through every subcell, so the smallest photocurrent limits the stack. Aim for roughly equal photocurrents (e.g. perovskite/Si: 14.67 vs 14.01 mA cm⁻²).

Learn it — ideal power (no double counting!)
Single: Pmax = q∫Eg∞φEdE × (Eg/q) × FF
Tandem: P = Eg1∫Eg1∞φEdE·FF₁ + Eg2∫Eg2Eg1φEdE·FF₂
The bottom cell only gets photons between Eg2 and Eg1. J(λg) = q∫₀λgφλdλ is a cumulative current, not the spectral Jλ.

Choosing gaps (3J): pick a bottom gap and read Jtotal off the cumulative curve. Set cutoffs at Jtotal/3 and 2Jtotal/3, convert λ → Eg (1240/λ), then compare the power. Equal currents are the starting rule, not the final optimum.

4Lattice + bandgap matching

  • Epitaxial growth needs similar lattice constants, otherwise strain causes defects and recombination. (Diamond Si doesn't match many III–Vs.)
  • Alloys tune both values. Lattice constant follows Vegard's law (linear). Bandgap bows:
Eg(x) ≈ (1−x)Eg,A + xEg,B − b x(1−x)
  • Lattice-matched (LM): GaInP / Ga(In)As / Ge. Compatible growth, but limited Eg choice. (Ge is cheap with a small gap.)
  • Metamorphic (MM): a step-graded buffer absorbs the mismatch. More Eg freedom, but defects must be controlled.
EQE graph: GaInP collects short λ, GaInAs the middle and Ge the IR. A high "total" EQE ≠ a high series current! Also: tandem efficiency rises with the number of junctions and with concentration.
Summary: MJ = big gap on top, tunnel junctions in between, matched currents, matched lattices. Calculate power band by band and don't count any photon twice.

Concentrator PV (CPV)

Use cheap large-area optics to focus sunlight onto a small, expensive, high-efficiency cell. There's a catch, and it's called series resistance.

1Concentrator types

Parabolic dish · parabolic trough · linear lens · central receiver ("power tower") · Fresnel lens.

2Principal considerations

  • Only direct (beam) radiation can be concentrated, so you need tracking. That means sunny, clear-sky (desert) sites.
  • Large cheap collector + small high-η cell.
  • VOC gain vs Rs loss (see right).
  • Non-imaging optics (CPC). No need to "focus" an image.
  • Practical issues: cooling, tracking accuracy, a good top-contact grid vs back-contact.
  • There is a maximum concentration ratio.

3Imaging vs non-imaging

4Why can't you concentrate diffuse light?

Diffuse light arrives from all directions (high directional disorder = high entropy). Squeezing it into a directional beam would decrease its entropy, which the second law forbids for passive optics.

Learn it — solar constant geometry
GSE = GS(RS/RSE)²
Irradiance falls with the square of the distance from the Sun. S = GSE.

5Concentration X suns

Learn it
ISC(X) = X·ISC(1)
VOC(X) = VOC(1) + (kBT/q) ln X
rs(X) = Rs·X·ISC(1)/VOC(X)
η(X) = ISC(1)[VOC(1) + (kBT/q)ln X]·FF₀[1 − rs(X)] / Pin(1)
X cancels top and bottom, except in the ln X gain and the rs(X) loss.
Efficiency vs concentration at 25 °C. The loss Ploss = J²Rs grows as X², the gain only as ln X.
Don't forget temperature! Concentrated light heats the cell, and VOC = … − β(T − TSTC) drops. That's why cooling is essential for CPV.
CPV loves high-η multijunction cells. The cell area is tiny, so you can afford the pricey III–Vs.

Cells are just converters. A system has to deliver enough energy, reliably, wherever it's installed.

Stand-alone: reliability, sizing, energy storage needed (intermittent). Grid-connected: monitoring, integration, tariffs.

2Peak Solar Hours (PSH)

Learn it
PSH = ∫24h G(t) dt / GSTC
Unit: hours. Numerically, daily irradiation in kWh m⁻² = PSH. E.g. 4.5 kWh m⁻² day⁻¹ = 4.5 PSH.
Edaily = P₀ × PSH   ⇒   P₀ = Edaily/PSH
P₀ = array size in Wp (peak watts at STC). It's power, not area or energy.

3Location & season

  • Sahara: lots of direct beam, steady all year. London: low in winter, a large diffuse fraction. Tropics: fairly flat but diffuse-heavy.
  • Concentrators → only where the direct beam dominates (deserts).
  • Higher latitude → bigger seasonal swing → bigger tilt and more storage.

e.g. 2000 → 2680 kWh m⁻² yr⁻¹ (+680, i.e. 34%). Cloudy sites gain less (less direct beam). The irradiation gain doesn't guarantee the same electrical gain.

Cells → Modules → Arrays → Grid

Wire cells in series for voltage and strings in parallel for current. Then protect, track and convert.

1Modules

  • Typically 36 cells in series (≈21 V VOC, made for 12 V battery charging).
  • VOC,module ≈ Ncell × VOC,cell;  ISC,module ≈ ISC,cell.
  • One weak or shaded cell limits the whole series string's current.
  • G changes ISC (∝ G). T changes VOC (falls). So the MPP moves around, and you need an MPPT (dP/dV = 0).

2Arrays & protection diodes

  • Blocking (string) diode, in series: stops reverse current from the battery or grid flowing back into a string at night or under shade.
  • Bypass diode, in parallel with a module or cell group: lets current go around a shaded/broken module, preventing reverse bias (hot spots) and reducing losses.

3Balance of system

  • Stand-alone: charge regulator (prevents over-charge and deep discharge, low-voltage disconnect) + battery (+ inverter for AC loads).
  • Battery charging without MPPT: the module is pinned at battery voltage, so Peff ≈ 12 V × ISC < Pmax.
  • Inverter: an H-bridge flips the DC polarity (S1+S4: +VDC; S2+S3: −VDC). PWM shapes the 50 Hz sine and an LCL filter removes the switching ripple. Dead time stops shoot-through.

WORKED EXAMPLE — 4 modules, one shaded (300 W each, the shaded one 150 W; 30 V; ηinv = 96%)
  • String, all active at 5 A: 4×30×5 = 600 W. Shaded one bypassed: 3×30×10 = 900 W → AC = 864 W
  • Microinverters: 3×300 + 150 = 1050 W → AC = 1008 W (+16.7%)

4Grid & Performance Ratio

The grid is high-V, 50 Hz (±1%), AC and needs a stable supply. PV is low-V, DC, intermittent and distributed. So you need inverters, synchronisation and anti-islanding.

Learn it
PR = Euse / (P₀ × PSH)
Euse = measured AC energy delivered. P₀×PSH = reference energy at STC (see IEC 61724). Typical PR ≈ 0.7–0.8.

PR losses: G below STC, high cell temperature (summer!), spectrum and incidence angle, MPPT/mismatch, degradation over the years, dirt and shading (snow in winter).

Module Performance at Operating Conditions (NOCT)

Datasheets quote STC values (1 kW m⁻², 25 °C). Real modules run hotter and dimmer, so convert to operating conditions (opc) before you design anything.

STC datasheet: ISC, VOC, Pmax, FF, NOCT, β+Measured: G, Ta→ ① Tc→② ISC→③ VOC→④ FF (via Rs)→⑤ Pmax  ⑥ η
Learn the six steps

① Cell temperature (NOCT measured at Ta = 20 °C, 800 W m⁻², wind 1 m s⁻¹, open circuit):

Tc = Ta + (NOCT − 20) × G/800

② Current:

ISC(opc) = (G/1000) ISC,STC

③ Voltage (per cell):

VOC = VOC,STC + (kBTc/q) ln(G/1000) − β(Tc − TSTC)

④ Fill factor:

voc = qVOC/kBTc    FF₀ = [voc − ln(voc+0.72)] / (voc+1)
FF = FF₀(1 − rs),   rs = RsISC/VOC

⑤ Power:

Pmax = Ncells × ISC × VOC,cell × FF

⑥ Efficiency:

η = JSCVOC,cellFF / G
Why measure NOCT at open circuit? At VOC no power is extracted. All the photogenerated carriers recombine and release their energy as heat, which is the worst-case heating. The (Tc − Ta) ∝ G relation is then just scaled from 800 W m⁻².
Read the question carefully: are the given values for a cell or a module? Divide the module VOC by Ncells before using the per-cell formulas!
WORKED EXAMPLE — the one to master
Given: 36 cells; STC Pmax = 85.0 W, VOC = 21.6 V, ISC = 5.00 A; NOCT = 45 °C; G = 800 W m⁻²; Ta = 30 °C; Eg/q = 1.12 V.
  1. Tc = 30 + 25×800/800 = 55 °C (328.15 K)
  2. ISC = 5.00×0.8 = 4.00 A
  3. VOC,cell,STC = 21.6/36 = 0.600 V
    β = (1.12−0.600)/298.15 = 1.744 mV K⁻¹
    kBTc/q = 0.02828 V
    VOC,cell = 0.600 + 0.02828 ln 0.8 − 0.001744×30 = 0.5414 V
    Module: 36×0.5414 = 19.49 V
  4. Fit Rs at STC: FFSTC = 85/(21.6×5) = 0.787; v = 0.600/0.02569 = 23.35 → FF₀ = 0.828
    Rs,cell ≈ (1 − 0.787/0.828)×0.600/5 = 0.00598 Ω
    At opc: v = 19.14 → FF₀ = 0.802; rs = 0.00598×4/0.5414 = 0.0442
    FF = 0.802×(1−0.0442) = 0.767
  5. Pmax = 36×0.5414×4.00×0.767 ≈ 59.8 W

That's only 70% of the 85 W label. Scaling for irradiance alone would give 68 W, so the heat costs another 8 W. An array of 5 in series × 2 strings: VOC ≈ 97.4 V, ISC = 8 A, P ≈ 598 W (DC, before losses).

!Measured vs modelled

Southampton array (Pmax = 850 W, 2 strings × 5 modules, fixed tilt, no MPPT): the modelled curves match the measurements in both winter and summer. Neither season reaches 850 W, because of G < 1 kW m⁻², high Tc and no MPPT.

Stand-Alone Systems: Sizing & Reliability

Off-grid, the PV array is the only energy source. Get the sizing wrong and the lights go out. So it's about array size, battery size and reliability.

1Sizing the array

Learn it
P₀(array) = D / PSH
Nmodules = P₀(array) / P₀(module) → round UP
D = daily demand (Wh). With system efficiency fsys: P₀ = D/(fsys×PSH). Stand-alone charging often uses Peff instead of Pmax.
WORKED EXAMPLE — remote cottage
D = 3.0 kWh day⁻¹, PSH = 5.0 h, module = 58 Wp
  1. P₀ = 3000/5 = 600 W
  2. N = 600/58 = 10.3 → 11 modules

2Battery & days of autonomy (DOA)

Site latitudeDays of autonomy
0–30°5–6
30–50°10–12
>50°15
Learn it
Battery size (Wh) = D × DOA    DOA = battery energy / D
Capacity (Ah) = D × DOA / Vbatt  (usually 12 V)
e.g. 3000 Wh × 5 days / 12 V = 1250 Ah.

3Oversizing

Oversize factor f = P₀(array) / P₀(needed) > 1
  • A sizing curve (from statistics) shows the trade-off: a bigger array ↔ smaller battery for the same reliability.
  • Oversizing makes more energy at low irradiance, compensates for degradation and future-proofs the system. Remote industrial systems are usually heavily oversized.
  • Bulawayo example: oversize 1.1 → only 2.7 DOA needed.

4The climate cycle

Charge-removal curve: the deepest dip tells you the battery capacity you need.

5Reliability metrics

Learn it
LOLP = tfault / ttotal   (dimensionless)
MTBF = ttotal / number of faults    λ = 1/MTBF
  • LOLP (loss-of-load probability): the fraction of time demand > supply. It's probabilistic and uses historical or simulated solar time series, so it only works if the radiation statistics are reproduced correctly. US utility benchmark: 0.1 day/yr, i.e. 0.1/365 ≈ 0.03% (about 1 day in 10 years). It ignores transmission and distribution outages.
  • MTBF (mean time between failures): arguably closer to what industry needs. Higher = more reliable, but it needs long records.
WORKED EXAMPLE — 20-year simulation, oversize = 1.0
  • Battery 3.7 DOA → faults in 5 years → MTBF ≈ 20/5 = 4 yr
  • Battery 4.2 DOA → 1 fault → MTBF ≈ ≥ 20 yr
A small increase in storage (+0.5 days) gives a big gain in reliability!
Practice Questions
  1. A telecom site needs 1.2 kWh day⁻¹ at 40°N with PSH = 4. Size the array (100 Wp modules) and a 12 V battery for 10 DOA.
  2. A system failed 3 times in 15 years, total outage 6 days. Find MTBF and LOLP.
